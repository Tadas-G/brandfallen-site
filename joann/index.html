Joann Had Its Best Year Ever. Then All 800 Stores Closed.

In 2020, Joann added more than 8 million customers and made about $212 million. Five years later, all 800 stores were gone and 19,000 people were out of work. This is the story of a Cleveland shop that became America's biggest craft brand, took on debt, and went bankrupt twice in under a year.

Full transcript

In 2020, the whole world was locked inside, and one store chain had the best year it had seen in a very long time. In nine months it added more than 8 million customers. It made about 212 million dollars in profit.

A year earlier, the same company had lost more than 500 million.

Five years after that boom, every one of its stores was gone. All 800. And 19,000 people were out of work.

The company was Joann. By the end of this video, you'll know why the best year in its history didn't save it, why it went bankrupt twice in less than a year, and what one counter at the back of every store had to do with it.

But first, you need to know how it started. And it didn't start with fabric.

Part one. The cheese shop.

Cleveland, Ohio, 1943. A group of German immigrant families opened a small shop. By one account, it sold imported cheese. Then a little fabric appeared on the shelves, and the fabric sold better. The cheese went. It became the Cleveland Fabric Shop.

Two of the founders' daughters were called Joan and Jacqueline Ann. Put them together and you get the name that would hang over hundreds of shopping centers. Jo-Ann.

By 1963, there were 18 stores. In 1969, the company went public. In 1994, it bought a southern rival called Cloth World, with 342 stores. By the late nineties, Joann was the biggest craft brand in America.

And if you ever went in, you know how it worked. You took your roll of fabric to a long counter at the back. Someone unrolled it, measured it, and cut it by hand, and they usually asked what you were making.

Remember that counter. It comes back.

For more than sixty years, the business stayed close to the families. In 2006, it hired its first chief executive from outside them. And in 2010, the company looked better than ever. Its stock hit a record high. And according to its filings, it had no debt.

None.

Hold on to that word.

Part two. The buyout.

In 2011, a private equity firm called Leonard Green and Partners bought Joann for 1.6 billion dollars.

This was a leveraged buyout. It works like buying a house with a mortgage, except that the house then owes the mortgage. The buyer borrows most of the money, and the debt lands on the company it just bought.

If you watched our video on Red Lobster, or Bed Bath & Beyond, you know how this feels. A company with no debt suddenly has a lot of it. Joann also had to pay steep annual management fees.

So think about that. It's 2011. You run fabric stores. The people who sew are getting older. Hobby Lobby is spreading across the country, and Amazon sells thread. You need to spend money. But now a big share of what you earn goes somewhere else first. In some years, interest alone topped 100 million dollars. What would you cut?

Stop. Look at what Joann cut.

Between 2011 and 2023, nine different executives rotated through the chief executive's office. Nine. Consultants from McKinsey were brought in to study the workforce, and layoffs followed. The number of people in each store began to shrink.

Remember that counter? It needed a person. Every cut of fabric took a person. And there were fewer of them. Customers would eventually describe waits of 45 minutes, just to get a piece of cloth cut. One former employee of 16 years told Fortune the staff cuts had started back in the 2010s.

People noticed. Then they drove to Hobby Lobby.

By 2019, the debt had reached a peak of 1.3 billion dollars. That year, the company lost 546 million. Moody's flagged its debt as distressed. Joann looked finished.

And then, the world shut down.

Part three. The miracle.

In March 2020, hospitals ran short of masks. And suddenly, millions of people wanted to do something with their hands.

They wanted fabric. They wanted elastic. They wanted sewing machines. There was one big chain with a store near almost everyone. Joann handed out free mask kits, and it argued that this made it an essential business. Not everyone agreed. Michigan forced its stores to close, and critics called the kits scraps from the clearance bin.

But the customers came anyway. In the first nine months of 2020, sales rose more than 24%, to 1.9 billion dollars. The chain that had lost half a billion dollars was suddenly making a fortune. For two years it was a Fortune 1000 company.

The chief executive, Wade Miquelon, believed something had permanently changed. People wanted to do more themselves, he said. And that was the moment to act.

In March 2021, Joann went back on the stock market. It raised 131 million dollars, to put toward nearly a billion dollars of debt.

Look at that again. The best moment in the company's modern history, and the money covered about a seventh of what it owed.

And Leonard Green was still the majority shareholder.

Now think about what the boom did. It didn't fix the counter. It didn't pay off the debt. It only made a broken business look healthy, right when everyone was watching.

Part four. The hangover.

Then the masks came off. People went back to offices, and restaurants, and the sewing machines went back in the closet.

Sales fell more than 8% in a year. Shipping goods in from abroad cost an extra 91 million dollars. Younger shoppers were buying their fabric online. Michaels and Hobby Lobby kept pushing. And the stock began to fall.

In October 2023, it dropped below one dollar. From 2021 to 2024, Joann lost 99% of its value. Miquelon was gone. The company was laying off office staff.

In March 2024, Joann filed for bankruptcy. It was a quick one, with a plan to wipe out more than 500 million dollars of debt. No stores would close. A judge approved it, and by the end of April, Joann was out again. Private, and supposedly fixed.

It wasn't.

Part five. Nine months.

Here's the part nobody saw coming. The first bankruptcy hurt the one thing a store cannot live without. Its shelves.

Suppliers had been burned. Some cut production, or stopped altogether. Stock on the shelves fell by more than 10%. Customers came in for a specific yarn, a specific color, and left with nothing. By the end, it owed 7.5 million dollars to the maker of a popular crochet kit, and 5.2 million to a sewing machine company.

On January 15th, 2025, nine months after leaving bankruptcy, Joann filed again.

In February, it said 500 of its 800 stores would close. Twelve days later came the real news. No buyer had been found to keep it going. A liquidator, GA Group, won the assets. Every store would close.

The people who had waited 45 minutes at that counter came back one last time. By the end of May 2025, it was over. 82 years after that shop in Cleveland.

And the name? In June, Michaels, its old rival, bought the Joann name and its house brands. Not one store.

So what really killed Joann?

You'll hear about private equity, and that was real. You'll hear about Amazon and Hobby Lobby, and they were real too. But go back to that first number. In 2020 it had more than 8 million new customers, all at once, the best chance a retailer could dream of.

They got to the counter, and there was nobody there to cut the fabric.

The debt took the people. The people were the store. And when the boom came, there was nothing left to catch it with.

Joann didn't die in 2025. It was cut in 2011, and it took 14 years to fall apart.

So who was really holding the scissors?
