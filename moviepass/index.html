They Had 3 Million Customers. So They Changed Their Passwords.

MoviePass sold a movie ticket every day for $9.95 a month, and three million people signed up. Then the company cancelled the passwords of its 75,000 most loyal customers, ran out of money on a Thursday night, and ended with two executives pleading guilty in federal court. This is the story told backwards, from the courtroom to the one number that made it impossible.

The plea.

The man in court was Ted Farnsworth. He ran the company that owned MoviePass.

A few months before him, the chief executive of MoviePass, Mitch Lowe, had pleaded guilty too.

Prosecutors said the two men lied to investors. They said the business had been tested. That it was sustainable. That it was powered by big data and artificial intelligence.

According to prosecutors, none of that was true. The cheap price was, in their words, a temporary marketing gimmick.

But the lie to investors is not the strange part. Go back five years.

The bankruptcy.

January 2020. The owner of MoviePass files for bankruptcy. Not the kind where you try to recover. The kind where everything is sold.

Four months earlier, the service had simply been switched off. Customers opened the app and there was nothing there.

So where did three million customers go? Go back one more year.

The passwords.

It's 2018. MoviePass has a problem. Some customers love it too much.

They go to the movies all the time. And every time they go, MoviePass pays the cinema the full price of the ticket.

So the company finds a solution. It was later described by the Federal Trade Commission.

It takes the 75,000 people who use the service most, and it cancels their passwords.

It tells them it has detected suspicious activity. Possible fraud.

There was no fraud. They were just going to the movies. Like the advert told them to.

Regulators said the chief executive ordered it himself, and chose the number.

There was more. The top fifth of users had to photograph their ticket stubs and send them in. Thousands were blocked when it didn't work.

And there was a hidden trip wire. If a group of heavy users cost too much in a month, they were quietly cut off.

Stop for a second. A company is sabotaging the people who like it most.

Why would anyone do that? Go back a few months.

The night it went dark.

July 26th, 2018. A Thursday. One of the biggest films of the summer is about to open.

People walk up to the cinema, open MoviePass, and nothing happens.

It isn't a bug. MoviePass has run out of money. It can't pay for the tickets.

That night, its owner borrows five million dollars in cash, just to switch the service back on.

That weekend, the new Mission: Impossible is blocked at the big chains anyway.

A company with three million paying customers couldn't afford a Thursday. To see why, go back one more time.

Nine ninety-five.

August 2017. MoviePass is small. About 20,000 subscribers the year before. And it isn't cheap.

Then a little-known data company buys control of it for about 27 million dollars. And it makes one change.

The new price is nine dollars and ninety-five cents a month. For a movie ticket every single day.

Think about that. In a big city, one ticket could cost more than the whole month.

If a customer went once, MoviePass could lose money. If they went twice, it lost more. The better the customer, the bigger the loss.

And customers came. 600,000 by October. A million by December. More than three million by the next June.

Every one of them was a bill.

The plan was that the losses would be worth it. The data would be valuable. Cinemas would share their profits.

The founder.

Go back to the very beginning. 2011.

A film executive called Stacy Spikes has an idea. A subscription for cinemas. Pay monthly, go when you want.

He builds it slowly, for six years. At a price that might work.

Then he sells control. The new owners cut the price. Soon after, he's fired.

And here is the last twist. After the bankruptcy, MoviePass itself was put up for sale.

In November 2021, a judge approved the buyer. It was Stacy Spikes. He paid less than 250,000 dollars for the company that had once had three million customers.

The lesson.

MoviePass didn't fail because people didn't want it. It failed because they did.

It sold a ten-dollar bill for five dollars, and called it growth.

And when the customers showed up to collect, it changed their passwords.

MoviePass found three million people who loved it, and could not afford a single one of them.
