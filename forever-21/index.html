The Landlords Bought Their Own Tenant. Forever 21 Still Died.

In 2020, a shopping mall company helped buy one of its own tenants out of bankruptcy for $81 million. Forever 21 had once sold more than $4 billion of clothes in a year. Then its biggest rival became its partner, and it still went bankrupt again.

Full transcript

In February 2020, a shopping mall company did something strange. It helped buy one of its own tenants out of bankruptcy.

The price was 81 million dollars, for a business that had sold more than 4 billion dollars of clothes in a single year.

By the end of this video, you'll know why the landlords stepped in, why one of the buyers later called it probably the biggest mistake he ever made, and why the company that seemed to destroy Forever 21 ended up owning part of it.

But first, you need to meet a man cleaning floors.

Part one. Eleven thousand dollars.

Do Won Chang and his wife, Jin Sook, came to the United States from South Korea in 1981. According to one account, he worked as a janitor, at a gas station and in a coffee shop.

On April 16th, 1984, they used 11,000 dollars of savings to open a 900-square-foot store on North Figueroa Street in Highland Park, Los Angeles. It was called Fashion 21. They sold clothes aimed at the city's Korean American community, using wholesale closeouts.

First-year sales were 700,000 dollars.

The name became Forever 21. And the family found a formula. Cheap clothes, new stock all the time, and space in the mall. Some reports say a typical store rolled over more than 20% of its stock in a week. If you wanted something, you bought it now, because next week it would be gone.

Look at the bottom of the yellow bags, and you'd find John 3:16 printed there, a sign of the family's Christian faith. Inside the company, this stayed a family business, with daughters Linda and Esther in senior roles.

Part two. Bigger.

In 2005, Forever 21 bought the Gadzooks chain for 33 million dollars. Between 2005 and 2007, the store count doubled to about 400. Sales went from an estimated 640 million dollars in 2005 to about a billion the next year.

And the stores themselves kept growing. In 2006 it opened a 40,000-square-foot showcase in Pasadena. The average store reached 38,000 square feet, about the size of a department store.

It also went global. Seven international stores in 2005 became 262 in 2015. In that peak year, sales hit 4.4 billion dollars.

Not everyone was cheering. By October 2007, Forever 21 had faced more than 20 lawsuits from designers. Trovata, Anna Sui, Diane von Furstenberg and others had filed complaints. Forever 21 had no designers on staff, only what it called designer merchants. In the one case that reached a jury, the jurors were split, there was a mistrial, and it settled. As of 2011, it had never been found guilty, and most cases had settled.

Still, the model was obvious. See what's selling, get something similar into stores fast, keep the price low.

Remember that sentence. It comes back.

Part three. Too much floor.

Remember Red Lobster, and the rent it couldn't escape? Forever 21 had its own version. According to one account of its court filing, the company had too much floor space on leases signed a decade or more earlier, and was paying about 450 million dollars a year in rent.

Then the sales started to go. Revenue fell to 3.4 billion dollars in 2017. In 2018 it began pulling out of countries, including the Netherlands, Thailand and Ireland. In 2019, global sales fell 32%.

On September 29th, 2019, Forever 21 filed for bankruptcy, with liabilities of between 1 and 10 billion dollars, and more than 100,000 creditors. It planned to leave 40 countries.

Stores in Canada, Europe and Asia were reportedly losing about 10 million dollars a month.

And by February 2020, it was running out of time. It reportedly had 5 million dollars in cash, and 24 million dollars of rent due that month.

Stop. Imagine you own the malls. One of your biggest tenants is about to disappear, and it leaves dark, empty boxes the size of department stores in your buildings. Empty space can hurt everything around it. You can let it die. Or you can buy it. What would you do?

Simon Property Group and Brookfield, two mall giants, teamed up with Authentic Brands. For 81 million dollars, they bought it.

Part four. The enemy moves in.

It worked for a while. The American business made 165 million dollars before interest, tax, depreciation and amortization in 2021, according to one account. But a new rival had appeared, and it made Forever 21 look slow.

Shein.

Remember the sentence. See what's selling, get something similar out fast, keep the price low. Now do that without any stores. According to one analysis, Shein introduced about 1.5 million products in the US in a single year, compared with about 40,000 at Zara.

It also had a tax advantage. Under the de minimis exemption, imports worth less than 800 dollars could enter the United States free of duty and tax.

So in August 2023, Forever 21's owners did something remarkable. Their operating group, SPARC, entered a partnership with Shein. Each took a stake in the other, and Shein acquired about a third of SPARC. Forever 21 clothes appeared on Shein's platform. Shein pop-ups appeared in Forever 21 stores.

The company that was eating its business was now its partner.

It didn't save anything. In January 2024, Authentic's chief, Jamie Salter, reportedly called buying Forever 21 probably the biggest mistake he had made.

Part five. Empty.

In February 2025, Forever 21 announced plans to close 215 underperforming locations. In March came about 700 layoffs, and the closure of the Los Angeles headquarters.

Then, in March 2025, the operator of the US business filed for bankruptcy for the second time, with liabilities of between 1 and 5 billion dollars. Its CFO, Brad Sell, said foreign fast-fashion companies had been able to take advantage of the de minimis exemption, and it undercut pricing and margin.

The company looked for a buyer. Nobody came. By May 1st, 2025, the American stores were gone, about 350 of them.

The first store that started it all had already closed, in 2020.

And today? The name lives on. Authentic owns the brand and licenses it. Forever 21 was relaunched online in September 2025. Its clothes have been sold through other retailers. The American mall stores did not come back with it.

So what really happened?

Shein was a real threat. The tax rule was real too. But Forever 21 was in bankruptcy years before Shein's partnership, and its trouble was its size. It built giant stores for a world of mall shoppers, and signed for them years in advance. Those leases couldn't change as fast as its clothes could.

The landlords didn't buy a fashion company. They bought time.

So did Shein really kill Forever 21? Or did Forever 21 simply teach the world how to replace it?
