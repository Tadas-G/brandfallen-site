One Rent Bill Doubled. 13 Months Later Barneys Was Gone.

In January 2019, one rent bill on Madison Avenue nearly doubled. Thirteen months later, Barneys New York was gone. This is the story of how a shop opened with a pawned ring became America's coolest store, and why the family that built it never owned the palace it built.

Full transcript

In January 2019, one rent bill on Madison Avenue nearly doubled. It went from about 16 million dollars to about 30 million.

Seven months later, the tenant was bankrupt. Thirteen months later, it was gone.

The tenant was Barneys New York, the store that brought Giorgio Armani to America.

By the end of this video, you'll know who really paid for that building, how Barneys lost it, and why the same family that built this empire ended up with almost nothing.

But first, you need to see how it started. Because it started with a ring.

Part one. The pawn shop.

New York, 1923. Barney Pressman needs 500 dollars to lease a shop. So, the story goes, he pawns his wife's engagement ring.

He gets 500 square feet at Seventh Avenue and 17th Street, and 40 brand name suits. This was no luxury store. Barney bought samples, overstock, and whatever was left at bankruptcy sales. His slogan was "No Bunk, No Junk, No Imitations."

He was also a born showman. He bought radio ads shouting "Calling all men to Barney's." He once chartered a boat and took 2,000 customers to Coney Island.

Keep that first detail in mind. A shop, and a lease. It comes back.

His son Fred joined in 1946, and Fred wanted something else. In the sixties, he began pulling the store away from discounts. He brought in Pierre Cardin, Christian Dior and Givenchy. In 1973 he said he was convinced the discount route was not for them.

Then, in 1976, came the move that changed American fashion. Barneys signed an exclusive deal with a designer most Americans had never heard of. Giorgio Armani.

That same year, Barneys began selling women's clothes. The next generation, Fred's sons Gene and Bob, pushed hard into it. In 1986, a 25 million dollar women's store opened on 17th Street, with cheeky windows by Simon Doonan.

A discount suit shop had become the coolest store in New York.

And it wanted to be bigger.

Part two. The palace.

It's 1989. You run the hottest store in Manhattan, but you're stuck downtown. The real money is uptown. A giant Japanese retailer, Isetan, offers to pay for your expansion. Hundreds of millions of dollars. Your own new stores in New York, Chicago and Beverly Hills.

There's one condition you might not think about right away. You won't own the buildings.

Would you take it?

The Pressmans took it. Isetan committed 236 million dollars to those three stores. The partners also planned to spend 250 million on 30 smaller Barneys shops, and in 1990 the first one opened in Tokyo. A suit shop from Seventh Avenue was now a global name.

Stop. Look at what they built.

In 1993, Barneys opened on Madison Avenue. 230,000 square feet, at a reported cost of 267 million dollars. Then came Beverly Hills, at 120 million.

They were beautiful. And the numbers underneath them weren't. By September 1995, Isetan had spent 616 million dollars on Barneys, including about 168 million in emergency loans. That year, Barneys lost 120 million dollars on sales of about 339 million.

In other words, for every three dollars that came through the door, one more was going out of it.

Isetan had had enough. The two sides fell out over the final money, and Isetan called it a breach of trust.

In January 1996, Barneys filed for bankruptcy. Isetan's claims alone came to 480 million dollars. The banks wanted nearly 170 million more, and others about 150 million. Stores in Dallas, Houston, Cleveland, Costa Mesa and Short Hills went dark.

Six months later, Fred Pressman died.

Part three. The landlords.

Here's what the family had really signed.

Barneys came out of bankruptcy in 1999. Isetan kept the three great buildings. The investors who had bought up the debt, at about 40 cents on the dollar, took around 70% of the company. The Pressmans kept about one and a half percent.

Seventy-six years after that ring, the family was nearly written out of its own store. In 2004, it sold what was left.

Now the building and the store were two different things. And that's the moment everything turned.

In 2001, Isetan sold the Madison Avenue and Beverly Hills stores to a property company called Ashkenazy. If you watched our video on Forever 21, you know landlords can decide the fate of a brand. In this case, Ashkenazy got something very specific. The right to raise the rent in 2019.

Remember that year.

Meanwhile, Barneys was passed around like a coat. Jones Apparel bought it in 2004. Three years later, Dubai's Istithmar paid about 937 million dollars, around 500 million of that in debt. Then came the financial crisis. By 2012, the hedge fund manager Richard Perry's firm took control, wiping out 540 million of 590 million in debt.

For a while, it looked like the fix had worked. Online sales grew from about 18 million dollars to 200 million. In 2016, Barneys even went home, to a new store on the same corner in Chelsea where Barney had started.

But nearly half of its sales still came through one building on Madison Avenue. A building it had paid for once, lost, and now rented.

Part four. The bill.

January 2019. The clock Ashkenazy had been holding for years finally ran out. Rent went from about 16 million to about 30 million.

Barneys protested, and lost. CNBC reported the increase nearly wiped out its earnings. Chief executive Daniella Vitale said the entire industry was in survival mode.

On August 6th, 2019, Barneys filed for bankruptcy again. Of its 22 locations, 15 would close, among them Chicago, Las Vegas and Seattle. Reports said brands like Celine, Balenciaga and The Row were still waiting to be paid. The store that once gave Armani his American stage now owed money to the people who filled its shelves.

There was one hope. A buyer who wanted to keep the store alive. Instead, on October 25th, the winning bidder was Authentic Brands Group, at about 271 million dollars. It's a company that manages brand names. The stores themselves would close.

And so the most elegant store in New York spent its last months as a liquidation sale. Sandwich boards where the holiday windows used to be. Shoppers picking through the racks.

On February 23rd, 2020, Barneys New York closed its doors for good. Almost 97 years after Barney's ring.

And the name? It went to Saks Fifth Avenue under license. Barneys became a floor inside someone else's store. Then in January 2026, Saks went bankrupt too. Those last American Barneys shut. Today, the biggest thing left of it is nine stores in Japan, the country whose money built the palace.

So who really killed Barneys?

You'll hear about online shopping, and it hurt. You'll hear about a rent bill, and that was the last blow. But that bill was written long before 2019. It was written in 1989, when a family that had built its fortune on knowing exactly what things were worth signed away the one thing that couldn't be replaced.

They built a palace and handed someone else the deed.

Barneys began with a lease. And a lease is what finished it.
