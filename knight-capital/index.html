Full transcript

August 1st, 2012. 9:30 in the morning. The opening bell rings in New York.

Inside an office across the river, a computer starts buying stocks. Nobody told it to.

It buys at the high price. It sells at the low price. Then it does it again. More than a thousand times a second.

Every minute, about ten million dollars disappears.

The people in the room can see it happening. They can't find what is doing it. And there is no off switch.

Forty-five minutes later, about 440 million dollars is gone.

The company was Knight Capital, the biggest trader of American stocks.

By the end of this video, you'll know the one decision that set this up years earlier, the 97 warnings nobody acted on that morning, and the moment they tried to fix it and made it worse.

The giant nobody knew.

Knight Capital started in 1995, in Jersey City, New Jersey. Most people had never heard of it. But if you bought a share through an online broker, there was a good chance Knight was on the other side.

It was a market maker. It bought from people who wanted to sell, sold to people who wanted to buy, and kept a tiny slice of each trade. A fraction of a cent, millions of times a day.

By 2012 it handled about 17% of all trading on the New York Stock Exchange, and almost the same on Nasdaq. It had around 1,400 employees.

People didn't do that work anymore. Software did. At Knight, the software that sent orders to the market ran on eight servers.

Remember that number. Eight.

The decision.

Years earlier, Knight's system had a function called Power Peg. It took one big order and chopped it into small ones, and kept sending them until the big order was filled.

Then Knight stopped using it.

And here is the decision. Nobody deleted it. The old code stayed on the servers, switched off, with its switch still wired in. It was easier to leave it there.

In 2005, engineers moved another piece of code. The part that counted how many shares had been filled. After that, Power Peg could no longer tell when to stop. But it was switched off, so nobody tested it.

It sat there for years. A machine with no brakes, waiting for someone to press the button.

The new code.

In the summer of 2012, the New York Stock Exchange launched a new program for small investors. It would start on August 1st. Knight wanted to be in it from the first day.

So its engineers wrote new code. And to turn it on, they reused an old switch. The same switch that used to turn on Power Peg.

On the new code, that was safe. The plan was to remove the old function first.

In the days before the launch, a technician copied the new code onto the servers, one by one. Seven of them got it.

The eighth did not.

Nobody checked. There was no second person to review the work, and no written rule that said there should be.

Seven servers had the new code. One still had Power Peg, and a switch about to be flipped.

97 warnings.

On the morning of August 1st, before the market opened, Knight's system began sending automatic emails to a group of staff. They pointed at the order system and named an error.

Ninety-seven of them.

The emails weren't built as alarms, and nobody treated them as one. Nobody acted on them.

Knight's chief executive, Thomas Joyce, wasn't thinking about servers. He'd had knee surgery the day before.

At 9:30, the bell rang.

45 minutes.

Orders came in from customers. Seven servers handled them properly. The eighth woke up Power Peg.

Power Peg started buying and selling. And because it could no longer count, it never stopped.

Knight had 212 real customer orders that morning. To fill them, its system sent more than four million orders into the market.

It traded in 154 different stocks. Nearly 400 million shares. Prices across the exchange jumped for no reason anyone could see.

Inside Knight, they knew within minutes that something was wrong. They didn't know what. There was no procedure for this, and no single button to shut it all down.

So they guessed. They decided the new code must be the problem. And they removed it from the seven servers where it was working.

Now the switch was still on, and the old function took over there too.

It got worse.

When they finally stopped it, around 10:15, Knight was holding billions of dollars of stocks it never meant to buy.

The bill.

Knight asked the regulator to cancel the trades. It was an obvious mistake, after all.

The answer was no. Trades were cancelled in just six stocks, where prices had moved 30% or more. Everything else stood.

Knight had to get rid of the rest fast. Goldman Sachs agreed to buy the whole pile, at a discount.

The final loss was about 440 million dollars. Regulators later put it above 460.

Knight's own shares fell 33% that day. Within two days, they were down more than 70%.

A firm that had taken 17 years to build had four days to find money, or close.

It found it. That weekend, six firms put in 400 million dollars. In return, they took about 73% of the company.

Knight survived as a name for a few more months. In December, it agreed to be taken over by one of its rescuers, a rival called Getco. In July 2013 the deal closed, and Thomas Joyce was out.

The regulator fined what was left 12 million dollars. Its report didn't blame a programmer. It blamed a company that had no checks on what it put into the market.

One switch.

Knight Capital didn't fall because of a bad bet, or fraud, or a crash.

It fell because of a piece of old code that someone decided not to delete. One server that someone forgot. And 97 warnings that nobody acted on.

Any one of those would've been harmless. Together, they cost ten million dollars a minute.

Knight Capital left a broken switch in its system for years, and one morning someone flipped it.
