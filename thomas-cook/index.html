The Night Thomas Cook Left 600,000 People Stranded

Just after 2am on September 23rd, 2019, the world's oldest travel company stopped trading. 600,000 customers were on holiday, and their flights home were gone. Four suspects have been blamed for killing Thomas Cook. This investigation tests each one against the evidence.

Full transcript

Just after two in the morning on Monday, September 23rd, 2019, Britain's aviation regulator put out a statement.

The world's oldest travel company had stopped trading. With immediate effect.

At that moment, around 600,000 of its customers were on holiday. About 150,000 of them were British. They had seats booked home on planes that would never take off.

Some of those planes were already stuck. Around midnight, as Thomas Cook jets landed back in Britain, airports began holding them on the ground over unpaid charges.

Hotels had been nervous for days. At one resort in Tunisia, guests said guards kept the gates shut while staff demanded they pay for their rooms again. Thomas Cook said only a small number were asked, and it refunded them.

By morning, about 21,000 people had lost their jobs. Nine thousand of them in Britain.

The company was 178 years old. It died in one night.

So what killed it? There are four suspects, and every one of them has been blamed. By the end of this video, you'll know which one actually did it. And you'll see why the British public paid for the rescue anyway.

But first, the victim.

The victim.

Leicester, England. July 5th, 1841. A cabinet-maker and former Baptist preacher called Thomas Cook talks a railway company into running a special train. About 500 people pay one shilling each and ride to a temperance meeting in Loughborough.

It has been called the first package tour. Thomas Cook had invented the holiday you buy in one piece.

For the next century and a half, his name meant travel. By 2019, the group sold holidays across Europe, flew its own airline, and had 555 shops on British high streets. In 2018, its revenue was about 9.6 billion pounds.

Within about a year and a half of May 2018, more than 96% of its share price was gone.

Something was eating it from the inside. Let's meet the suspects.

Suspect one. The internet.

This is the obvious one. People stopped walking into travel agents. They booked a flight on one website and a hotel on another.

The evidence is real. In March 2019, Thomas Cook said 64% of its bookings in 2018 had been made online. It announced 21 shop closures.

But the internet didn't arrive in 2019. Thomas Cook had a website. It had its own planes and its own hotel brands. And other package holiday companies, facing the same internet, were still flying the morning after Thomas Cook stopped.

The internet hurt. It doesn't explain one night.

Suspect two. The weather.

Thomas Cook had its own explanation. In May 2019, it blamed "an uncertain consumer environment." Brexit, and the long, hot European summer of 2018. When it's hot at home, fewer people pay to go somewhere hot.

Stop. Think about that for a second. Heatwaves come and go. Brexit hit every travel company in Britain. A bad summer can ruin a year. It doesn't normally finish off a company that survived two world wars.

And that same month, Thomas Cook published its half-year results. It had lost 1.5 billion pounds in six months.

1.1 billion of that had nothing to do with the weather.

Hold on to that number. 1.1 billion. We'll come back to it.

Suspect three. The government.

By the summer of 2019, Thomas Cook had a rescue. In August, the Chinese group Fosun agreed to put in 450 million pounds, as part of a 900 million pound deal with the company's lenders.

Then, in the final days, the banks asked for more. Another 200 million pounds, so the company could get through the quiet winter months.

Less than a week before the end, Thomas Cook asked the government to cover that gap.

So imagine you're the minister. A private company, run by its own executives, wants 200 million pounds of public money. Say yes, and every struggling company in the country will be at your door. Say no, and 150,000 people are stuck abroad.

What would you do?

The government said no.

And then it paid anyway.

The rescue was called Operation Matterhorn. It became the biggest peacetime repatriation in British history. Planes were hired from easyJet and Virgin Atlantic, and even a giant Malaysia Airlines A380. Over about two weeks, around 140,000 people were flown home.

In March 2020, the National Audit Office added up the cost to taxpayers. At least 156 million pounds. And the fund that protects package holidays faced claims of around 481 million more.

So was the government the killer? Not quite. Because the real question is whether 200 million pounds would have saved Thomas Cook, or just bought it one more winter. To answer that, we need the last suspect.

Suspect four. The debt.

Go back to 2007. Thomas Cook merged with a rival called MyTravel. The deal promised savings of more than 75 million pounds a year. The chief executive at the time, Manny Fontenla-Novoa, reportedly received a 5 million pound bonus after it.

The merger also came with debt. A lot of it.

In November 2011, the bill arrived for the first time. Thomas Cook went back to its banks for more money, and its shares fell about 75% in a single day. The banks agreed to lend it 200 million pounds. The price was steep. Extra fees, and an interest rate set to double.

200 million pounds. Remember that. Eight years later, it's exactly what the company would be begging for again.

Thomas Cook survived 2011. The debt never left. In its final years, it owed more than a billion pounds. Its last chief executive, Peter Fankhauser, later said his rescue plan would have cut the yearly interest bill from about 150 million pounds to 50 million.

150 million pounds a year. Just to keep up with what it owed. Whatever the weather.

And that 1.1 billion pound loss from May 2019? Most of it was a write-down. The company was admitting, in its own accounts, that the MyTravel deal from 2007 was worth far less than it had paid.

Meanwhile, the people at the top were paid. MPs found that directors received about 20 million pounds in bonuses in the company's last five years, based on profits that left out the huge one-off costs. Fankhauser was paid about 8.4 million pounds in his time as boss, though much of it came in shares that ended up worthless.

The verdict.

So let's put the evidence on the table.

The internet made the business harder. The weather made one summer worse. The government chose not to step in at the very end. Every one of them pushed.

But only one suspect was there in 2011, and still there in 2019. Only one took around 150 million pounds a year, every year. Money that could never go into fixing anything else. That was the debt. And it was signed in 2007.

The heatwave didn't sink Thomas Cook. It just showed how little room was left.

Six weeks after the collapse, Fosun, the company that had planned to put in 450 million pounds, bought the Thomas Cook name and logo for 11 million. Another travel firm, Hays Travel, took over all 555 shops.

If you watched our video on Spirit Airlines, you saw an airline repossessed in the dark. Thomas Cook was the same kind of night, on a much bigger stage. 600,000 people asleep in their hotels while their way home disappeared.

It took 178 years to build Thomas Cook. And one deal to break it.
