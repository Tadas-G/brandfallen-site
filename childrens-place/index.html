Just In: The Children's Place Files for Bankruptcy. Who Killed It?

On Sunday, November 8th, 2026, The Children's Place filed for Chapter 11 bankruptcy. 514 stores, 881 million dollars of debt, and one month to find a buyer. Its own website now says all stores are closing. This is an investigation with four suspects: the mall, the giants, tariffs and the rescue that was meant to save it.

Full transcript

Sunday, November 8th, 2026. Most of America is watching football.

In a federal court in New Jersey, a company quietly files for bankruptcy.

By Monday morning, its website has a new banner. All stores closing. Everything must go.

Five hundred and fourteen stores. A clothing brand that dressed American kids for 57 years.

It owes 881 million dollars. And it has one month to find someone willing to buy what's left.

The company is The Children's Place. And this happened days ago.

So who killed it? There are four suspects. By the end of this video, you'll know which one actually did it, and which one everyone is wrong about.

The victim.

The Children's Place opened in 1969, in Hartford, Connecticut. Two founders, one shop, selling toys and clothes.

They soon saw where the growth was. The clothes. Kids grow. Every few months, you need the next size.

That was the whole business. Cheap, cheerful, and you were always coming back.

It grew into one of the biggest children's clothing chains in America. In 2019 it even bought the name of its fallen rival, Gymboree.

In January 2018, one share cost almost 150 dollars.

By August 2026, the same share cost two dollars and forty cents.

Something took 98% of it. Let's meet the suspects.

Suspect one. The mall.

The Children's Place lived in shopping malls. And you know what happened to malls.

Fewer people walk through them every year. Reports on this bankruptcy point straight at it. Falling store traffic.

But here is the problem with this suspect. The Children's Place knew. It spent years closing stores and pushing people online.

Then its online sales got weaker too.

The mall hurt it. The mall didn't kill it.

Suspect two. The giants.

Think about where you'd buy a kid's T-shirt today. Amazon. Walmart. Target. In one click, or in the same trip as the groceries.

A store that only sells children's clothes has to give you a reason to make a special trip. For decades, the reason was price.

Then everyone else got cheaper.

This suspect was in the room. But giants have been selling kids' clothes for years. Why did it die now?

Suspect three. Tariffs.

Almost everything The Children's Place sells is made overseas. When import taxes went up, its costs went up with them.

In 2025, the company said it had a plan to cancel out about 80% of that hit.

It didn't hold. Tariff increases are named among the causes of this collapse. So are shoppers who simply spent less.

In the three months to August, sales fell almost 19%. The loss was 31 million dollars. A year earlier, it had been five.

Tariffs pushed it. But a healthy company survives a push. This one had nothing left to lean on. And that brings us to the last suspect.

Suspect four. The rescue.

Go back to February 2024. The Children's Place tells investors it has a liquidity problem. In plain words, it's running out of cash.

And then someone arrives. A family office from Saudi Arabia, called Mithaq Capital. Without being invited, it buys up more than half of the company.

Then it lends. 78 million dollars, with no interest. Then 90 million more.

It looked like a rescue. And for a while, the lights stayed on.

But a loan is not a cure. The stores were still emptying. The sales were still falling. And now there was more to repay.

Look at the final numbers. 742 million dollars in assets. 881 million in debt.

The company was worth less than what it owed.

Days before the filing, its interim chief executive and its strategy chief both stepped down.

The verdict.

So who killed The Children's Place?

Not the mall alone. Not Amazon alone. Not tariffs alone.

It was a company that had been bleeding for years, and was given a loan instead of a fix. The first hard push finished it.

And the people who pay are not in the boardroom. A warehouse in Alabama is closing. 593 jobs. Suppliers are owed millions.

What happens next is still open. Bids are due on December 9th. Someone may buy the name. The stores are another question.

The company says only certain stores will close. Its own website says all of them.

The Children's Place sold the next size up for 57 years, and ran out of time in a single weekend.
