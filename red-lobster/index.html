Endless Shrimp Cost $11 Million. The Rent Cost $190 Million.

Everyone says endless shrimp killed Red Lobster. The reported cost of the deal was about $11 million. The same year's lease bill was about $190 million. So what really happened, and why are creditors now in court over who wanted that deal?

Full transcript

Twenty dollars. All the shrimp you could eat, every day, for as long as you could keep going.

In 2023, Red Lobster made that deal permanent. Less than a year later, the company was in bankruptcy court, and the internet had its answer. Red Lobster was killed by shrimp. It's a great story. People laughed about it for months.

There's just one problem with it. The reported cost of that shrimp deal was about 11 million dollars. And in the same year, Red Lobster's lease bill was about 190 million.

So what really happened to one of the best-known restaurant chains in America? Why were its restaurants paying rent on buildings the chain used to own? And why are the people it owes money to now in court, arguing about who wanted that deal in the first place?

To answer that, we have to go back to a small Florida town.

In January 1968, Bill Darden and Charley Woodsby opened the first Red Lobster in Lakeland, Florida. Two years later, General Mills bought the company. And then it took off. Twenty-four restaurants by the end of fiscal 1971. By 1976, 174 of them in 26 states. In 1988, it passed one billion dollars in North American sales.

For a lot of Americans in the middle of the country, this was where you first ate lobster. It was the place for a birthday, an anniversary, a big night out.

In 1995, General Mills spun off its restaurants into a new company, Darden. For the next two decades, Red Lobster sat inside that company. It wasn't exciting anymore, but it had something valuable that nobody was talking about. It owned a lot of land.

By 2014, Red Lobster was slipping. It had 706 locations across the US and Canada, and it brought in about 30% of Darden's revenue. But same-store sales had dropped 8.8% in one quarter. In December 2013, Darden had said it wanted to sell or spin off the brand. Investors in Darden were furious at how it was being handled.

On May 16th, Darden agreed to sell Red Lobster to a private equity firm, Golden Gate Capital, for 2.1 billion dollars. An activist investor called it a fire sale. Another said the deal undervalued the chain and its real estate. Remember that second part.

Here's the part that matters. On the same deal, Golden Gate arranged a separate sale. About 500 restaurants' worth of real estate went to a property company for 1.5 billion dollars. In other words, most of the purchase price could be raised by selling the buildings under the restaurants.

Red Lobster, which hadn't been paying rent on that real estate, was now a tenant. A material portion of those leases were later described in the bankruptcy as above market. Rent was expected to rise 2% every year. Forever is a long time when your sales are falling. And that bill would still be there years later.

Would you buy a restaurant chain and then sell the floor it stands on? It's a common private equity move, and it can work, as long as the business keeps growing.

Next came the supplier. Thai Union, the seafood giant, had sold to Red Lobster for over 20 years. In October 2016, it paid 575 million dollars for a 25% stake. Then, in August 2020, in the middle of the pandemic, Golden Gate sold the rest of its stake to a group led by Thai Union.

Thai Union had said it wouldn't be involved in day-to-day operations. In November 2022, its chief financial officer said it was much deeper involved in the management of Red Lobster.

Now look at the top of the company. CEO Kelli Valade arrived in 2021 and left within a year, after a reported dispute with board member Paul Kenny. In July 2022, the chief operations officer and six operations vice presidents were fired. A Thai Union frozen seafood manager with no restaurant experience became COO. And Kenny became interim CEO.

Meanwhile, customers were leaving. According to the bankruptcy filing, customer count was down about 30% since 2019. Losses of about 33 million dollars were reported for 2022.

That was before anyone had touched the shrimp deal.

So here's the question. If you're losing customers, and you want them back, what do you do?

For nearly 20 years, endless shrimp had been a limited-time promotion. In 2023, according to reports of the bankruptcy filing, it became a permanent 20-dollar menu item, over management's pushback.

It worked. That's what people forget. Traffic rose 3.7% in June, 4.3% in July, and 10.7% in August.

And the people who came kept eating.

Thai Union's finance chief was blunt on an earnings call. Quote: "We knew the price was cheap." The price went to 22 dollars, then 25. A later CEO said it created chaos operationally.

The reported cost came to about 11 million dollars in one quarter. That's real money. It also isn't what buries a chain with over 2 billion dollars in revenue. Put the two numbers next to each other. Eleven million for the shrimp. About 190 million a year in lease obligations, with 64 million of that on underperforming restaurants.

Here's where the story gets darker, and from here on, we're talking about allegations, not findings.

The bankruptcy filing said Thai Union had an outsized influence over shrimp purchasing, and suggested the deal may have been more about boosting Thai Union's own sales. In 2026, a trust acting for creditors went further and sued. It alleges that Thai Union's people used the shrimp deal to enrich themselves, and that a longtime supplier was pushed out so Thai Union could take more of the business.

Thai Union rejects all of it. It calls the suit a cynical attempt to blame a former owner. It says Red Lobster's managers came up with the deal, and that nobody invests 575 million dollars to win about 33 million in shrimp sales. As far as we can find, no court has ruled.

Either way, in January 2024, Thai Union announced it was getting out, and estimated a non-cash write-down of about 530 million dollars.

By spring, the cash was going fast. It fell from 100 million dollars to under 30 million in six months. By March, Red Lobster was paying its bills an average of 48 days late. Talks to fix it out of court failed. In May, Red Lobster closed 93 locations. On May 19th, 2024, a Sunday, it filed for bankruptcy. At that moment it had 551 restaurants in the US and 27 in Canada, and about 36,000 employees, most of them part-time. This was a chain that served 64 million customers a year, and it was in court.

The filing didn't name one killer. It listed several: competition and the economy, too many restaurants, that real estate burden, a revolving door of CEOs, a concentrated shrimp supply, and yes, endless shrimp. The restructuring CEO said the promotion played a role.

Played a role. That's a long way from the headline.

Red Lobster left bankruptcy on September 16th, 2024, owned by its lenders, led by Fortress. Its new CEO, Damola Adamolekun, was 35 years old, and he said he had inherited a very damaged brand.

Today, Red Lobster is still alive, with 484 US locations as of September 2026. Compare that with those 706 in 2014, although that older number included Canada. In June, the Times Square restaurant closed after 23 years. The company says it's tracking ahead of forecasts. Bloomberg has questioned the turnaround.

And in April 2026, endless shrimp came back. This time for a limited time, at 24 dollars and 99 cents.

So the deal that supposedly destroyed the company is back on the menu, and the rent still has to be paid.

Here's what the evidence points to. The shrimp didn't sink a healthy company. It was the last, loudest thing to happen to one that was already taking on water. A sold-off foundation, a shrinking customer base, and a churn of leaders had done their work for years.

But nobody tells a funny story about a sale-leaseback.

So did shrimp really sink Red Lobster?

Or did it just become the perfect scapegoat?
