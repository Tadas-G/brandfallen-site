Bed Bath & Beyond Spent $11.8 Billion on Itself. Then It Went Bankrupt.

When Bed Bath & Beyond went bankrupt it owed about $5.2 billion. Over the years before that, it had spent about $11.8 billion buying back its own stock. This is the story of a company with no long-term debt that borrowed to buy its own shares, and of a name later sold for $21.5 million.

Full transcript

In April 2023, Bed Bath & Beyond filed for bankruptcy. Its last quarterly filing showed about 5.2 billion dollars of debt.

Now here's the number that doesn't fit. In the years before that, the company had spent about 11.8 billion dollars buying back its own stock.

More than twice its debt. Gone. Not on stores, and not on a website. On its own shares.

By the end of this video, you'll know why a company with no long-term debt chose to do that, what the money actually bought, and how the name ended up being sold for 21.5 million dollars. Then you'll see the strangest part. Bed Bath & Beyond is back.

But first, you need to meet two men with a very small idea.

Part one. Bed 'n Bath.

Warren Eisenberg and Leonard Feinstein had both worked in management at a discount chain called Arlan's. In 1971, they opened a store of their own in Springfield, New Jersey. It was called Bed 'n Bath.

The idea was simple. Sell the nice things for the home, and sell a lot of them, in one place.

By 1985 they had about 18 stores, in the New York area and California. That same year, they opened their first superstore. It was about 20,000 square feet, more than ten times the size of the first shop. The old name didn't fit any more. In 1987 it became Bed Bath & Beyond.

In 1992, the company went public on the Nasdaq. Its 100th store opened in October 1996. Its 200th opened in August 1999. That same year, sales reached a billion dollars for the first time.

And if you lived in America, you knew about the coupon. Big, blue, and in your mailbox. Twenty percent off one item. They came so often that people saved them up for the next trip.

Remember that coupon. It comes back.

By 2011, there were 1,142 stores. In 2018, there were about 1,550, and yearly sales of around 12 billion dollars.

From the outside, it looked unstoppable. Inside, a decision had already been made.

Part two. The buyback.

Starting in 2004, Bed Bath & Beyond began buying its own shares.

A buyback sounds technical, but it's simple. A company takes cash, buys its own stock from investors, and cancels it. There are fewer shares, so each one owns a bigger slice. Earnings per share go up, even if the business itself hasn't grown at all.

For years, this looked sensible. The company was making real money. It had no long-term debt. What else was it going to do with the cash?

Here's what else. Amazon was growing. People were starting to buy towels on their phones. And Bed Bath & Beyond's website was far behind.

Then the buybacks grew. In July 2014, the company sold 1.5 billion dollars of bonds, and said the money was for buying more of its shares. 1.1 billion went straight into one buyback.

Stop. Think about that for a second. You run a chain of stores. The internet is coming for you, and you know it. And you borrow a billion and a half dollars. Not for a warehouse, not for a website. To buy your own stock. What would you have done with that money?

CNN added it all up. From 2004 to early 2022, the company spent about 11.8 billion dollars on buybacks. The columnist Allan Sloan put the average price above 44 dollars a share.

Hold on to that number. 44 dollars.

Part three. The fix.

By 2019, investors had run out of patience. Three activist firms, Legion Partners, Macellum Advisors and Ancora Advisors, moved to remove the chief executive, Steven Temares. In April, five independent directors stepped down. In May, Temares left.

In November 2019, a new chief executive arrived. Mark Tritton had been the chief merchandising officer at Target, and he had a plan that had worked there. Build your own brands. Own your own products. Keep the margin.

So store managers began stocking shelves with at least 10 company-owned brands. Popular national brands, the names shoppers came in for, began to disappear.

And the promotions were cut back too. Fewer of those coupons.

Think about what the customer saw. The store they knew, the one with the brands they wanted, and the blue coupon in the mail, was turning into something else. And the same products were a click away.

The buybacks didn't stop, either. Under Tritton, the company spent 625 million dollars on its own shares in 2021.

Then the shelves started to empty. Relying on factories for its own products left the company exposed when supply chains seized up. In the first quarter of 2022, sales fell 25%. The quarterly loss was 358 million dollars.

Mark Tritton was out by the middle of that year. Three of those new brands would soon be scrapped.

Part four. The meme.

And then, something very odd happened. The stock became a game.

In early 2022, the investor Ryan Cohen, chairman of GameStop, took a 9.8% stake and won three seats on the board. His online followers piled in behind him. The share price began to jump, for reasons that had little to do with selling towels.

In August 2022, the shares surged. Then a filing appeared. Cohen had moved to sell. Within days, the stake was gone. Benzinga calculated his profit at about 68 million dollars. The stock collapsed.

So now look at the company. Sales were falling. Cash was running out. In January 2023, it warned of substantial doubt that it could keep going. Its banks cut its credit line.

In February, a financing deal with Hudson Bay Capital promised to raise more than a billion dollars. Many analysts saw it as buying a little time. By March, the company said it would likely go bankrupt unless it could sell 300 million dollars of stock. The deal ended.

Do you remember that number? 44 dollars a share?

By the time it went bankrupt, the stock was worth less than a dollar.

Part five. Beyond.

On April 23rd, 2023, Bed Bath & Beyond filed for Chapter 11, with plans to wind the business down. Its last quarterly filing had shown assets of about 4.4 billion dollars, and debts of about 5.2 billion.

Three days later, it stopped accepting coupons.

Those blue coupons, the ones people had been saving for years, were finished. Rivals smelled the opening. Big Lots offered to take them. So did The Container Store. Remember that name too. Liquidation sales began the next day. On July 30th, 2023, the last stores closed. Fifty-two years after that first shop in New Jersey.

The shareholders' stock was cancelled that September.

And the name? In June 2023, Overstock.com bought it at auction for 21.5 million dollars.

Think about that price. The company had spent more than 500 times that buying its own shares. Overstock then changed its own name. On August 1st, 2023, the online retailer became Bed Bath & Beyond. No stores. Just the name.

Its boss said there was no plan to reopen them. Then he added, never say never.

That brings us to today. In April 2026, the new Bed Bath & Beyond agreed to buy The Container Store, the rival that once took its coupons, for about 150 million dollars. Container Store locations began changing over to a combined format in May. And that spring, it was reported to be bringing back the coupons.

The stores are back. The coupon is back. Only the company is gone.

So what really killed it?

You'll hear about Amazon, and that was real. You'll hear about the private brands, and those hurt too. But think about what those problems needed. They needed money. Money for a website, for stores that looked new, for time.

And that money had already been spent. It didn't go into a warehouse or a website. It went to shareholders, one buyback at a time, while the business that paid for it all fell behind.

Remember Rite Aid, and the debt it could never escape? Bed Bath & Beyond had a choice that Rite Aid didn't. It started with no long-term debt at all.

It spent nearly 12 billion dollars proving how much it believed in its own stock.

So what was the real price of that belief?
