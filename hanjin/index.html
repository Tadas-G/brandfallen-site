The Ships No Port Would Let In: Hanjin Shipping

September 2016. More than 90 ships, by one count, were stuck at sea with around 14 billion dollars of cargo, and no port would let them in. This is the story of Hanjin Shipping, told backwards: from the stranded ships to the decisions that sank South Korea's biggest container line years before anyone noticed.

Full transcript

September 2016. Somewhere off the coast of Singapore, a container ship sits at anchor. It's not allowed into port. Its 24 crew members are not allowed to go ashore. The food on board is running out, so another ship pulls up alongside and hands some over.

The ship is called the Hanjin Rome. And it's one of dozens.

All over the world, ships painted with the same name were circling outside harbours that wouldn't let them in. By one count, more than 90 of them were stuck at sea. Inside them sat around 14 billion dollars of other people's cargo. Goods for shops all over the world, weeks before the holidays.

Nobody would unload them, because nobody knew who was going to pay.

This was Hanjin Shipping. South Korea's biggest container line, and one of the ten biggest in the world. And in a single week, it had gone from moving the world's goods to being turned away from the world's ports.

By the end of this video, you'll know how it got there, which decision sank it years before anyone noticed, and why the person running it sold her shares just before the bad news broke.

To understand it, we have to go backwards.

2016.

On August 30th, 2016, Hanjin's banks, led by the state-run Korea Development Bank, said they were done. The rescue plan from Hanjin's parent group was not enough. The bank's chairman said it lacked conviction.

The next day, August 31st, Hanjin filed for court receivership in Seoul.

And the ships were already at sea.

The night before the filing, the Hanjin Rome was arrested in Singapore by a creditor. In California, the Hanjin Montevideo was held over a fuel bill of 488,750 dollars. Ports were afraid to let Hanjin ships dock, because a docked ship can be seized. And nobody knew if the dockworkers, the tugboats or the fuel would ever be paid for.

On September 2nd, a court in New Jersey gave Hanjin's ships protection, so they could unload in America without being seized. Slowly, cargo started to come off.

Shops felt it anyway. Retailers had stock stuck at sea right before Thanksgiving, Black Friday and Christmas. Big brands could wait. Smaller companies, with one shipment for the whole season, were hit hardest.

On February 17th, 2017, a Korean court declared Hanjin bankrupt. It was the largest bankruptcy in the history of container shipping. Months later, its trustee had raised about 220 million dollars from its assets. Against debts of about 10.5 billion. Two cents on the dollar.

Its last remaining ship was the Hanjin Rome. A Singapore court sold it for scrap.

But the debt that sank it wasn't new in 2016. So go back a few months.

April 2016.

Hanjin asks its banks for a creditor-led restructuring, a last try to avoid court. The shares fall hard.

Now look at who sold just before.

Days before that announcement, the former chairwoman, Choi Eun-young, and her two daughters sold their Hanjin shares. Prosecutors said they avoided about a billion won in losses.

She said she sold the shares to repay loans. The courts disagreed. She was convicted of insider trading and sentenced to 18 months in prison, and South Korea's Supreme Court upheld it.

So who was Choi Eun-young? And why was a former chairwoman still holding shares in a company she no longer ran? To answer that, go back two more years.

2014.

By 2014, Hanjin was already in deep trouble. Choi handed control to her brother-in-law, Cho Yang-ho. He ran the whole Hanjin Group, including the national airline, Korean Air.

In June 2014, Korean Air bought a third of Hanjin Shipping. In the end, the group put in about 1.2 trillion won, more than a billion dollars, trying to save it.

It wasn't enough. Because the real problem was written into contracts that couldn't be torn up.

Hanjin didn't own most of its ships. By 2016, it owned only 44% of its container fleet. The rest were rented from other companies, under charters that ran for years.

And it owed its landlords at sea a fortune. In its final year, it faced around 1.3 trillion won in charter and terminal fees that it simply couldn't pay. The banks demanded it cut those costs by up to 30%. The shipowners said no.

Stop. Look at this one contract.

In the years after 2010, Hanjin rented seven giant container ships, each built for 10,000 standard containers. 43,750 dollars a day, per ship. For ten years.

That's more than 100 million dollars a year, for seven ships, whether they were full or empty.

Now imagine you're signing that. Freight prices have crashed, and you're betting they'll bounce back soon. If you're right, you're ahead of everyone. If you're wrong, you're locked in for a decade.

Would you sign it?

Hanjin signed. And the prices didn't come back.

2010.

In 2010, Hanjin was the first to bring a ship that size into service. Its fleet nearly doubled between 2009 and 2013.

On paper, it looked like a company growing into a giant. Underneath, the debt was growing faster.

2008.

This is the moment it began.

Before the crash, shipping rates were climbing. Rivals like Maersk were ordering bigger and bigger ships. Hanjin didn't want to fall behind. In 2008, it ordered new ships with room for 250,000 containers.

Less than two months later, the financial crisis hit. World trade fell off a cliff. And Hanjin had a fleet on order for a world that had just disappeared.

Every decision after this, the charters, the borrowing, the rescue money, was about paying for a bet made in 2008.

2006.

And who made those bets?

In 2002, the founder of the Hanjin Group died. His eldest son, Cho Yang-ho, took the airline. His third son, Cho Soo-ho, took the shipping company.

In 2006, Cho Soo-ho died of lung cancer. His widow, Choi Eun-young, took over Hanjin Shipping. At a hearing in 2016, she told the court that, as a housewife, she hadn't been prepared to run it, and that she bore part of the blame.

Her years in charge were the years of the big orders and the long charters.

1945.

And all of it started with one truck.

In 1945, Cho Choong-hoon started a transport business with a single truck in the port city of Incheon. It became the Hanjin Group. In 1977, the group founded Hanjin Shipping.

From one truck to one of the biggest fleets on earth. In two generations.

So now go forward again, and watch it all fall.

A family business passes to a son, then to his widow. She expands the fleet at the top of the market. The crash comes two months later. To keep growing, the company rents ships it can't afford, for years it can't escape. The family's airline pours in more than a billion dollars. The banks finally say no. And on the last day, ships carrying 14 billion dollars of cargo float outside ports that won't let them in.

If you watched our video on Thomas Cook, you saw 600,000 people stranded by one company in one night. Hanjin did it to the goods instead.

The storm didn't sink Hanjin. The contracts did.
