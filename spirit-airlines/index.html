Spirit Airlines Stopped Flying at 3 A.M. The Repo Crews Were Already There.

In 2015, Spirit Airlines earned $317 million. On May 2, 2026, it stopped flying at 3 a.m. while repo crews were already moving for its jets. This is how America's most complained-about airline got rich, and what happened when everyone copied it.

Full transcript

At six in the evening on May 1st, 2026, the repo crews started moving.

They weren't coming for cars. They were coming for jets. One of the men running the job later said he had six hours to find 20 pilots.

Nine hours after those crews started, at 3 a.m. Eastern, Spirit Airlines stopped existing as an airline. About 17,000 direct and indirect jobs went with it, according to the carrier. Its app and website said all flights were cancelled and customer service was no longer available.

Here's the strange part. Twenty-seven months earlier, a federal judge had looked at a plan to sell Spirit for about 3.8 billion dollars, and he stopped it. His reason was that travelers who depended on Spirit's cheap fares would be hurt.

So how does the company a judge tried to protect end up being repossessed in the dark?

And how did America's most complained-about airline get there, when for years it was also one of the most profitable?

If you've ever paid for a seat and then paid again for the bag, you've met this business. In a way, you helped build it.

The airline started small. In 1983, a man named Ned Homfeld founded Charter One, a Detroit operator flying tour groups to places like Atlantic City and Las Vegas. In May 1992 it got jets and a new name, Spirit. Its first scheduled route ran from Detroit to Atlantic City. In December 1999, it moved its headquarters to Miramar, Florida.

For a long time, it was just another small airline. Then came the man who changed it. Ben Baldanza joined as president in 2005. In 2006, Indigo Partners invested, and Baldanza became CEO.

They looked at Ryanair in Europe and borrowed a simple, ruthless idea. Sell the seat for almost nothing. Charge for nearly everything else.

In 2010, Spirit became the first American airline to charge for a carry-on bag.

Think about what that means. Most airlines sold you a trip. Spirit sold you a seat, then a list of options, and the ticket price you saw on the search page looked unbeatable. That number was the whole game. Spirit could appear at the top of the list and collect the rest later.

Customers hated it. In 2013, Spirit drew 9.43 complaints per 100,000 passengers, by one consumer group's count of federal data. The rest of the industry mostly stayed at two or less. Spirit's answer was that many of those complaints came from people who didn't understand its unbundled fares. Then it leaned in, and turned the hatred into a branding opportunity.

And the money came in.

The company had gone public in May 2011 at 12 dollars a share, at the low end of a range that had already been cut. Investors were cautious. Four years later they looked foolish. In 2015, Spirit earned 317 million dollars on about 2.1 billion in revenue. Its operating margin was 23.8%, the highest in its history. It was the company's ninth profitable year in a row.

One headline summed it up: the most hated airline in America was also the most profitable.

So what could beat an airline like that? Nobody had to beat it. They only had to copy it.

Spirit's trick lived on that search page. It worked because the big carriers looked expensive. So the legacy airlines built their own bare-bones fares, and put them on the same screen. A traveler could now see a cheap ticket from a major airline, with a bigger network behind it. Spirit's main weapon had become a feature on everyone else's menu.

Then costs moved. After the pandemic, airlines raised pay to attract pilots, and a business built on being the cheapest has very little room for that.

Spirit went looking for a partner. In February 2022 it agreed to merge with Frontier, another low-cost carrier. The combined airline would have been the fifth largest in the country.

Then JetBlue walked in with cash. Spirit's board said no at first. JetBlue kept pushing, and in July 2022, Spirit's shareholders rejected the Frontier deal, and JetBlue agreed to pay 33 dollars and 50 cents a share. It seemed like the perfect ending. The most hated airline would be bought, repainted, and disappear on good terms.

But the Justice Department sued. Its argument was that the deal would mean higher fares and fewer seats. The trial began in October 2023.

The airlines said they needed to combine to compete with the giants that control most of the market. On January 16th, 2024, Judge William G. Young ruled against them. The takeover, he wrote, would harm cost-conscious travelers who rely on Spirit's low fares.

Spirit's stock fell about 47%.

This is the turning point, so look at what the ruling assumed. It treated Spirit as a strong competitor worth keeping. The market reaction said something else.

On March 4th, 2024, JetBlue and Spirit ended the deal, and the shares closed at a record low of 5 dollars and 76 cents. Remember, the IPO was at 12. Spirit had received 425 million dollars in prepayments from JetBlue during the agreement, and another 69 million came with the termination. It was real money, and it didn't fix anything.

Because something else had happened in the meantime, and it had nothing to do with the judge. A defect in Pratt & Whitney engines grounded dozens of Spirit's Airbus jets for inspections. An ultra-low-cost airline only works when its planes are in the air. Every parked jet still has to be paid for, and it sells no seats at all.

Spirit hadn't made a profit since 2019.

On November 18th, 2024, it filed for Chapter 11 bankruptcy. It blamed mounting losses, failed mergers, debt and competition. Its shares were delisted.

By March 2025 it was out again, after swapping 795 million dollars of debt for equity. That sounds like a rescue. Look closer. The airline avoided the larger changes that might have saved it, like getting rid of planes and shrinking hard. Analysts later said it had dodged the hard decisions.

It lasted about five months.

In the second quarter of 2025, Spirit lost nearly 246 million dollars. The company warned it might not make it through another year. On August 29th, it filed for bankruptcy again.

Now it had no choice. It moved to get rid of 114 aircraft, roughly half its fleet. Pilots were furloughed, and November capacity was cut by about a quarter. When the numbers for 2025 were done, Spirit had taken in about 3.8 billion dollars and lost about 2.76 billion.

Then, in 2026, jet fuel prices surged. For an airline with no cushion, that was the final push.

There was one last chance. An eleventh-hour rescue proposal from the federal government could have been worth up to 500 million dollars. In return, the government would have stood ahead of other bondholders and taken a stake of up to 90%. The bondholders rejected it, and told the board the end was near.

So the airline ran its own funeral.

It cancelled international flights first, so that no planes or crews would be trapped abroad. On the last full day, it flew more than 50,000 people. At about 11:30 that Friday night, senior managers told operations staff in Orlando that it was over.

The last Spirit flight was NK1833, from Detroit to Dallas. It landed shortly after midnight with 175 passengers on board.

Detroit. That's where this all began, with a charter operator flying people to Atlantic City, 43 years earlier.

By morning, terminals had gone quiet and travelers were staring at cancelled flights across the US, the Caribbean and Latin America. Rivals offered capped rescue fares, most of them around 200 dollars. Spirit said most customers who paid by card were refunded by that evening.

The crews who'd started at 6 p.m. kept working. In just over a week, repo teams flew 23 Spirit jets to storage airports in the Arizona desert, some of them with former Spirit pilots at the controls.

So who killed Spirit Airlines?

It's tempting to blame the judge. He stopped the one deal that would have given the airline a safe exit. But he was asked one question: would this sale hurt travelers? And the rest of the story happened without him. The engines, the pay rises and the copied fares would all have landed on whoever owned those planes.

Here's the best explanation for what sank it. Spirit built a business on a single advantage, being the lowest number on the screen. When that number stopped being unique, it had no second idea. It had a small network, few loyal customers, and a name it had taught people to hate. Back when it was making 317 million a year, none of that mattered. When the bad years came, nobody was on its side.

Its customers were always loyal to one thing: the price. So the day someone else matched that price, they left.

In September 2026, the last 27 jets Spirit owned were disposed of in bankruptcy. There's no airline left to sell.

Plenty of companies get rich by being the cheapest option. Very few survive once they stop being the only one.

And that is how the most hated airline in America came to an end. Repossessed in the night, thirty-four years after it took the name Spirit.
