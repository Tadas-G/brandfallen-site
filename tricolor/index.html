Prosecutors Say This Car Lender Sold the Same Loans Twice

In August 2025, a phone call between two executives of a Texas car lender was secretly recorded. Three weeks later the company was gone. Prosecutors say Tricolor promised the same car loans to more than one bank: about $2.2 billion pledged, about $1.4 billion that really existed. This is the story of the company Daniel Chu built, and of what prosecutors and regulators say was underneath it. Daniel Chu has pleaded not guilty and has not been convicted.

Full transcript

August 2025. A phone call between two executives of a Texas car lender. The call is being secretly recorded.

The company has a problem. Its lenders have been asking for an audit since February, and the audit still isn't finished.

According to prosecutors, the chief executive says: where we would have an issue is if they sent an auditor.

The other man answers. Yes. That would be bad.

A few days later, prosecutors say, the same chief executive compares the situation to Enron. Enron obviously has a nice ring to it, right?

Three weeks after that, the company is gone.

The man on that call is Daniel Chu, founder of Tricolor. In December 2025, federal prosecutors in New York charged him with running a financial crimes enterprise. That count alone carries a mandatory minimum of ten years, and a maximum of life in prison.

Prosecutors say his company promised the same car loans to more than one bank. About 2.2 billion dollars of loans pledged. About 1.4 billion dollars that really existed.

An 800 million dollar hole.

Daniel Chu has pleaded not guilty. He has not been convicted of anything. This is the story of the company he built, and of what prosecutors and regulators say was underneath it.

The promise.

2007, Dallas. Daniel Chu starts a company that does two things. It sells used cars. And it lends people the money to buy them.

His customers are people most lenders turn away. Low credit, or no credit at all. The company focuses on lower-income Hispanic communities across the American southwest. And according to the indictment, it often lends without a credit check.

That sounds reckless. But think about who is on the other side of that loan.

In much of Texas, Arizona or California, there is no train to work. No bus that gets you there on time. If you don't have a car, you don't have a job. And if you don't have a credit history, a normal bank won't give you a car.

Tricolor fills that gap. And it grows.

By 2023, according to court filings, it has about 65 retail centers in six states. Texas, California, Nevada, Arizona, New Mexico and Illinois. At its peak, more than 1,500 people work there. Revenue reaches about a billion dollars a year.

Tens of thousands of families are driving a Tricolor car and paying a Tricolor loan every month.

In 2019, the US Treasury gives Tricolor a special designation, as a lender that serves communities the big banks ignore.

It looks like the rare business that does good and makes money at the same time.

The money.

There is a catch in this model. A lender needs money to lend.

Every car Tricolor sells on credit is money going out today, and coming back slowly, over years. To keep selling, it has to borrow.

So it does what lenders do. It goes to banks and borrows against its car loans. The loans are the collateral. If Tricolor can't pay, the bank can take the loans and collect the payments itself.

It also bundles thousands of those loans together and sells them to investors as bonds. Between 2020 and 2025, according to the SEC, Tricolor raises more than 1.9 billion dollars this way.

The names on the other side are some of the biggest in finance. JPMorgan Chase. Barclays. Fifth Third.

And every one of those deals rests on one simple promise. This loan is yours. Only yours. Nobody else has a claim on it.

Imagine you own one house and take out two mortgages on it, from two different banks, and each bank thinks it's the only one. That is what prosecutors say Tricolor did. Again and again.

The gap.

According to the indictment, it started around 2018.

Prosecutors say that, at Chu's direction, Tricolor repeatedly pledged the same collateral to multiple lenders. One loan, two owners. Sometimes more.

They say loans that had stopped paying were made to look current. That loan data was changed so that almost worthless loans appeared to meet the banks' rules. The SEC says these dead loans were sold into investors' bonds as if they were healthy.

And each bank, looking only at its own books, saw nothing wrong.

That is how a hole of 800 million dollars can stay hidden. Each lender sees its own pile of loans. Nobody puts all the piles side by side.

Nobody, that is, except an auditor.

The audit.

In February 2025, Tricolor's lenders start an audit.

By August, it still isn't finished, and the lenders start asking why.

This is when that phone call happens. The one where, prosecutors say, Chu worries about an auditor, and his finance chief agrees it would be bad.

According to the Justice Department, Chu then proposed various lies to get the company through it. Prosecutors also quote him describing Tricolor as basically history, and definitely insolvent.

And then, prosecutors say, something else happens.

On August 19th and 20th, Chu receives two payments from Tricolor totalling 6.25 million dollars. They are the final installments of a 15 million dollar bonus.

On or about August 27th, according to the indictment, he uses some of that money to buy a multimillion dollar property in Beverly Hills.

The fall.

Then it all happens in days.

Fifth Third, one of the lenders, announces it has found alleged fraud at a client. It doesn't name the client.

On September 6th, Tricolor puts more than a thousand employees on leave.

On September 10th, it files for bankruptcy. Not the kind where a company reorganises and survives. Chapter 7. Liquidation. Tricolor stops existing.

Its lenders are owed more than 900 million dollars. Tens of thousands of drivers still owe money on their cars. Their loans are now handled by a servicer appointed by the court.

The banks start counting. JPMorgan Chase writes off 170 million dollars. Fifth Third and Barclays each write off more than 100 million dollars.

And Tricolor isn't alone. The same month, an auto parts giant called First Brands collapses too, with billions of dollars that creditors say they can't find.

Two companies in the same industry, in the same month, with the same kind of questions about who really owned what.

JPMorgan's chief executive, Jamie Dimon, puts it in one sentence. When you see one cockroach, there's probably more.

The man.

December 2025. Federal agents arrest Daniel Chu, and the indictment is unsealed in New York.

Two of the other executives charged with him have already pleaded guilty, and they are cooperating with prosecutors.

In June 2026, Tricolor's chief operating officer pleads guilty too. He says he may testify against Chu.

In August 2026, the SEC brings its own case. It wants the money back, fines, and a ban on Chu ever running a public company again.

Daniel Chu is the only one of the four still saying he is not guilty. A jury has not heard his case. Everything you heard about what he said and did comes from prosecutors and regulators, and he has the right to answer it in court.

The lesson.

Tricolor's idea was not crazy. Lending to people the system ignores can be a real business, and for many people a car is the difference between having a job and not having one.

But a lender's whole business is a promise. Every loan exists. Every loan belongs to one bank.

If prosecutors are right, Tricolor didn't fall because its customers stopped paying. It fell because the people at the top stopped keeping that promise, and spent years making sure nobody would check.

One loan can only belong to one bank. Prosecutors say Tricolor forgot that for years, and the banks found out all at once.
