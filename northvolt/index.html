$13 Billion, One Week Left: How Northvolt Collapsed

November 2024: Northvolt, the company meant to be Europe's answer to China in electric-car batteries, told a US court it had 30 million dollars left, about one week of cash, against 5.8 billion in debt. It had raised about 13 billion. This is the story of Peter Carlsson, the former Tesla executive who built it, and the one thing his company never managed to do.

Full transcript

November 21st, 2024. A company that had raised about 13 billion dollars walks into an American bankruptcy court.

It tells the court it has 30 million dollars left in the bank. Enough to keep going for about one week.

It owes 5.8 billion.

The next day, the man who started it steps down.

The company was Northvolt. For years, it had been Europe's answer to China in the race to make batteries for electric cars. Volkswagen owned a fifth of it. Goldman Sachs invested. BMW signed a two-billion-euro order. Governments offered it money and land.

And the man at the center of it all had come from the most famous electric car company in the world.

By the end of this video, you'll know what he promised, the one thing his company never managed to do, and why that one thing mattered more than all the money.

This is the story of Peter Carlsson.

The man from Tesla.

Peter Carlsson was a Swedish supply chain executive. Before Northvolt, he worked at Tesla, on the side of the business that makes sure parts and materials arrive where they're needed.

He saw something from the inside. Electric cars were coming. And the battery was the most important part of the car. Whoever made the batteries would hold the power.

Most of those batteries were made in Asia. And Europe, home to Volkswagen, BMW, Mercedes and Volvo, depended on them.

So in 2015, Carlsson and another former Tesla executive, Paolo Cerruti, started a company in Sweden. Two years later it took the name Northvolt.

The pitch was powerful. Green batteries, made in Europe, with clean Swedish hydroelectric power. A European champion.

And Europe wanted to believe it.

The money.

In 2017, the European Investment Bank lent it 52 million euros for a demonstration plant. Two years later, it lent 350 million more.

In June 2019, Volkswagen put in about 900 million euros for around 20% of the company and a seat on the board. Goldman Sachs came in. So did BMW.

In July 2020, BMW signed an order worth two billion euros, for batteries to be delivered from 2024.

Northvolt had customers before it had a working factory. That's normal in this business. You sell the future, then you build it.

The question is how fast you can build it.

The factory.

Far in the north of Sweden, in a town called Skellefteå, Northvolt built its first gigafactory. Northvolt Ett.

In December 2021, the first battery cells came off the line. The plan was 16 gigawatt hours a year by 2024, enough for about 300,000 electric cars, and eventually 60.

But making a battery cell is not like making a car. A single cell is layers of chemistry thinner than paper, rolled and sealed with almost no room for error. Asian manufacturers had spent decades learning how to make millions of them without waste.

Northvolt was learning on the job. Reports said it struggled with quality from the start. It couldn't make its own cathode material good enough, the core chemical inside the cell, so it bought it from China.

The company built to be Europe's answer to China was buying a key ingredient from China.

The expansion.

Now here's the decision that defines Peter Carlsson.

Stop. Imagine you're him.

You have one factory. It's running, but it's not running well. Customers are waiting. And investors are still offering you billions of dollars.

Do you slow down and fix the one factory you have?

Or do you build more, before someone else takes the market?

Northvolt built more.

A plant in Poland. A lab in Västerås. A planned plant in Borlänge. A giant new plant in Heide, Germany, after the European Commission approved 902 million euros of state aid. And another one in Canada, near Montreal.

In August 2023, it raised another 1.2 billion dollars. In the first nine months of that year, it lost about 1 billion. In January 2024, it raised 5 billion more through green loans.

All in, by most counts, Northvolt raised about 13 billion dollars in debt and equity.

If you watched our video on Hanjin Shipping, you've seen this before. A company expanding at full speed, betting that the hard part would sort itself out later.

The cancellation.

It didn't.

In June 2024, BMW cancelled its two-billion-euro order. The reason, according to Reuters: Northvolt couldn't deliver on time.

Think about what that meant. The order that proved Northvolt was real, from one of the most demanding customers in the car industry, was gone.

And it said something every other customer could hear.

In September 2024, Northvolt cut 1,600 jobs. It stopped expanding its main factory. Carlsson admitted the company had been a little too aggressive in its expansion plans.

A little.

The week.

Two months later came the court filing.

Thirty million dollars in cash. 5.8 billion in debt. One week of runway.

Northvolt filed for Chapter 11 in the United States, hoping to restructure. The day after, Peter Carlsson stepped down. The company said it needed up to 1.2 billion dollars to survive.

Among the people it owed were the same names that had believed in it. Volkswagen. Goldman Sachs. Volkswagen's own brands, Scania, Porsche and Audi, were customers.

The money never came.

On March 12th, 2025, Northvolt filed for bankruptcy in Sweden. It was called the largest bankruptcy in modern Swedish industrial history.

By June, production in Skellefteå had stopped. Of around 5,000 workers, about 1,700 were kept on to look after what was left.

The investors lost heavily. A Canadian pension manager wrote off about 400 million dollars. Swedish investors lost more than half a billion. Governments that had pledged money took losses too.

In August 2025, an American company called Lyten agreed to buy what remained in Sweden and Germany. It plans to restart production, one line at a time.

The lesson.

So what went wrong?

It wasn't the idea. Europe really did need its own batteries. It still does.

It wasn't a lack of money. Northvolt had more money than almost any startup on the continent.

It was the order of things.

Peter Carlsson came from Tesla, a company famous for moving fast and fixing problems later. But battery cells don't forgive speed. Every percent of waste on the line is money burned, and Northvolt was trying to learn that lesson in five countries at once.

It sold the future before it could deliver the present. And when its biggest customer stopped waiting, the future was all it had left.

Northvolt tried to build Europe's battery industry before it could run a single factory.
