5,000 Stores Gone. Rite Aid Was Dying for 25 Years.

More than 5,000 stores. 63 years. Then one message on a website. Most people blame opioids or rivals, but Rite Aid's real problem started long before the last store closed.

Full transcript

In October 2025, a message went up on the Rite Aid website. All Rite Aid stores have now closed. We thank our loyal customers for their many years of support.

Sixty-three years. More than 5,000 stores at its peak. Gone.

Most people will tell you opioids killed Rite Aid. Or Amazon. Or CVS. By the end of this video, you'll know the decision that really did it, and why it happened more than 25 years before the last store closed.

Because this company had something very strange in its past. In 2000, Rite Aid erased 1.6 billion dollars of its own net income. At the time, the SEC called it by far the largest restatement ever by a public company.

So how does a chain survive that, and then still die?

Part one. The family business.

On September 12th, 1962, a man named Alex Grass opened a store in Scranton, Pennsylvania, called Thrift D Discount Center. Six years later, the company went public under a new name, Rite Aid. Two years after that, it was on the New York Stock Exchange.

Ten years in, it had 267 locations in 10 states. By 1983, it had hit a billion dollars in sales.

Then, in March 1995, Alex Grass stepped down. His son, Martin, took over.

Martin Grass wanted size, and he wanted it fast. In November 1995 he tried to merge with Revco, a deal worth 1.8 billion dollars, and it fell through after an antitrust challenge. So he went shopping elsewhere. In October 1996, Rite Aid bought Thrifty PayLess, a West Coast chain of 1,000 stores. In July 1997, it bought Harco and K&B on the Gulf Coast.

On paper, this looked like a triumph. In January 1999, the shares reached a high of 51 dollars and 12 cents.

Part two. The numbers that weren't real.

By the end of that same year, the stock had fallen to 4 dollars and 40 cents. The bonds were cut to junk. Six hundred stores were being closed. And on October 19th, 1999, Martin Grass stepped down as chairman and CEO.

What had happened?

In 2000, Rite Aid went back through its books. In July and October, it restated its cumulative pretax income by 2.3 billion dollars.

In June 2002, the SEC charged Grass, the former chief financial officer and the former vice chairman with what it called one of the most egregious accounting frauds in recent history.

Stop. Look at this one year. In fiscal 1999, the SEC said pretax income was inflated by at least 214 million dollars. A real loss of 14.7 million had been turned into a profit.

One trick was the vendor rebates. The SEC said 75 million dollars was booked, 42 million of it on the very last day of the fiscal year. That was 37% of the year's reported pretax income.

The money trail got personal too. The SEC said 2.6 million dollars went to a partnership connected to Grass and a relative, which bought an 83-acre site. After press reports, he paid 2.9 million back from his own account.

There's a smaller detail that tells you even more. The SEC said Grass told the company's lead bank that a finance committee had approved a stock pledge, and signed minutes of a meeting that never happened.

Grass was reportedly sentenced in 2004 to eight years in prison.

The fraud was over. The debt wasn't. And that is the part most people miss.

Part three. The bet.

New leaders pulled Rite Aid back from the edge. In July 2000 it sold its PCS Health Systems unit for a billion dollars, just to stay alive. Then, in 2006, came a decision you need to think about.

Imagine you run a drugstore chain still carrying heavy debt. Your rivals are bigger. Someone offers you 1,850 stores for 3.4 billion dollars in cash, plus stock. Do you take it, and become huge? Or do you stay small, and pay down what you owe?

Rite Aid took it. It bought the Eckerd and Brooks stores from Jean Coutu, and the deal closed in June 2007.

By 2008, Rite Aid had 5,059 stores and 112,800 employees. It was the biggest it would ever be.

Part four. The rescue that never came.

Remember Spirit Airlines? A deal blocked in court, and a company left to fend for itself. Rite Aid's version started on October 27th, 2015, when Walgreens agreed to buy it for 9.4 billion dollars.

The deal seemed like an escape. Regulators wanted stores sold first. The price was cut. And on June 29th, 2017, the merger was cancelled. Instead, Walgreens bought 1,932 stores for 4.38 billion dollars, and left Rite Aid to carry on alone with what remained.

Rite Aid's next idea was a merger of equals with Albertsons, the grocery chain, in February 2018. It was called off that August, after shareholders failed to approve it.

And there was a bitter footnote. In October 2023, Walgreens agreed to pay 192.5 million dollars to settle an investor suit over the failed bid. Rite Aid got none of that.

So now Rite Aid was smaller, still in debt, and competing with the same giants that had just bought its stores. It kept trying to buy its way forward. In 2015 it had paid about 2 billion dollars for a pharmacy benefits company, EnvisionRx. In October 2020, it bought Bartell Drugs, a 67-store Seattle chain, for 95 million dollars.

Part five. The bill.

Then came the lawsuits. Rite Aid faced a huge wave of opioid cases. In March 2023, the Justice Department filed a civil complaint alleging the chain had ignored red flags on prescriptions. Rite Aid fought that in court, and later reached a settlement.

There was stranger news too. In December 2023, Rite Aid settled with the FTC over facial recognition technology in its stores, and accepted a five-year ban. The FTC's complaint said the system had falsely identified shoppers.

By then, on October 15th, 2023, Rite Aid had filed for bankruptcy with about 4 billion dollars of funded debt. The stock hit an all-time low of 13 cents. Hundreds of stores closed.

In August 2024, 778 locations were closed or announced for closure, 38% of the chain. Every store in Michigan went. So did all but four in Ohio. That September, Rite Aid came out of bankruptcy, with about 2 billion dollars of debt eliminated, owned by its former creditors.

And less than a year later, it was back.

On May 5th, 2025, Rite Aid filed for bankruptcy again, with about 1,250 stores and about 24,500 employees. An executive told the court these setbacks had created a perfect storm. Vendors had become reluctant to ship, and promised capital hadn't arrived.

This time, nobody was saving it. CVS, Walgreens, Giant Eagle and others bought prescription files. By July, 1,184 locations were closed or liquidating, and only 56 stayed open. Store by store, the lights went out. Customers even had a deadline, June 5th, to use their gift cards and rewards. The Thrifty ice cream business, which came with that 1,000-store deal from 1996, sold for 19.2 million dollars.

The last locations, in Washington and Oregon, passed to CVS. In October, the message went up.

So here's the answer I promised.

The lawsuits were real. So were the rivals. But Rite Aid had been carrying debt from buying growth for decades. The fraud hid the problem. The Eckerd deal doubled down on it. Every rescue that followed needed someone else to take that debt away, and nobody did.

A company can survive a scandal. What's much harder to survive is the debt that came from trying to be bigger than you could afford.

Sixty-three years. And in the end, was it ever really the drugstores that failed? Or just the bill?
